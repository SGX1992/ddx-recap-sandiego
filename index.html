In brief

One day, three stages, one question

If AI can produce “good enough” design on demand, what is the job of a design and product leader now? DDX’26 San Diego put that question to twenty-five leaders from Adobe, Google, IDEO, Webflow, Otter.ai, Target, Netflix, Careem, TaylorMade, Marvin, Dscout and the Obama White House, and to a room that came to argue back.

The answer that emerged was consistent across talks, panels, workshops and the nine table talks that ran through the breaks: the tools raised the floor, not the ceiling. Taste, judgment and a durable point of view are the scarce resources. Research has to become always-on. And design is moving out of the interface and into decisions, organisations and the experience the business itself has become.

Don Norman opened the day with a fireside chat on the state of humanity-centred innovation. The closing panel put three enterprise design leaders on one stage to compare the expectations of the role with its reality in 2026. In between, the programme deliberately ran research, craft and strategy side by side, so that a golf-equipment AI feature, a Fortune 500 design organisation and a career sandbox sat in the same hour.

The floor rose. The ceiling didn’t move.

Good is free now. Great is the job.

The most-repeated argument of the day came from two stages at once. Richard Ward called the “death of design” narrative backwards: when everyone can clear the bar for good, the differentiator is knowing what great looks like and having the taste, judgment and craft to reach it. Kevin Wong went further: AI has not changed design, it has exposed how little depth most organisations were designing with. His framework, recognition (taste plus judgment) and commitment (a durable point of view), was echoed at the tables, where Jaleesa Chagan framed the edge as a shift “from capability to conviction”.

Research stops being a project and becomes a pulse.

Prayag Narula’s case for always-on research set the tone for the Forum stage: when teams ship weekly, episodic studies arrive after the decision. Harriet Swan and Katie Niles showed what that looks like in practice with TaylorMade’s AI caddie, CHIP: exploratory research to decide what it should say and do, A/B tests to find the features that moved engagement, and evaluative research to catch what the tests alone could not, including the inconclusive results. Bhavik Gandecha’s table talk put it in one line: AI moves fast, research still has to lead.

Design leaves the screen: decisions, systems, organisations.

Chris Gielow’s workshop asked designers to stop designing screens and start designing decisions, and to measure their value by the influence they have on what gets built, not by the deliverables they produce. Twisha Shah-Brandenburg took the same move one level up: AI transformation succeeds or fails on fragmented systems, invisible labour and unclear ownership, so the design brief is now the organisation itself. On the closing panel, Lauren Hughes, Stephanie Mencarelli and Kevin Wong compared the expectations placed on enterprise design leaders in 2026 with the reality of the role.

The human stays irrational, and irreplaceable.

Don Norman opened the day on the state of humanity-centred innovation; Rowan Salama closed the loop with the reminder that the rational user most products are designed for does not exist, and that AI’s best use is to understand the “beautifully irrational” ways people actually decide. Stephanie Mencarelli argued that as machines generate more, human originality becomes the scarce resource worth protecting, which is why her talk made joy, not productivity, the metric. Kara Fitzpatrick’s version on the culture panel: AI can read the deck, it cannot read the room.

Context is the asset. The experience is the business.

Two ideas from the tables and the late-afternoon stage belong together. Diana Wolosin’s “context engineers” talk argued that models and tools change weekly while well-structured knowledge compounds; AJ Azzi’s MCP roundtable showed the same thing from the tool side, with AI reaching into the systems teams already work in. Scott Robinson asked who we design for when an agent is the one browsing and buying. And the Experience Economy panel, with Ann Kostopanagiotou, Twisha Shah-Brandenburg, Diana Wolosin and Dave Rowley, drew the conclusion: digital experiences are no longer a layer on the business, they are the business.
